# ADR 0084: The app is also in Japanese, written to read naturally and open to correction

- Status: Accepted
- Date: 2026-10-21
- Amends: [ADR 0052](0052-the-app-language-is-chosen-behind-a-gear.md) (a language that few people who speak it have read says so in the language setting)
- Relates to: [ADR 0034](0034-tutorial-images-from-the-site-source-commit.md) (the guide's pictures are made in each language the app has), [ADR 0051](0051-a-language-of-the-guide-may-leave-pages-in-english.md) (the Japanese guide, and the notice that it was translated with AI help), [ADR 0053](0053-what-is-said-outside-the-window-is-a-code.md) (the sentences said by code), [ADR 0054](0054-the-about-tab-and-links-that-open-by-name.md) (the About tab and the links that open by name)

## Context

The app speaks English and Brazilian Portuguese, and ADR 0052 left room for a third: "Japanese is one more entry when its catalog exists". The guide has been in Japanese since 2026-10-16, and it names the controls in English because the app does; its home page says its pictures are of the English application. A person who reads manga on a Kindle, a Kobo or KOReader and reads Japanese is served by the guide and then meets an app in English.

Two things set Japanese apart from Portuguese.

- **Nobody who maintains the app can review it.** The repository already says that parts of it were written with AI help and asks for reports of mistakes (the README's AI disclosure), and the Japanese guide says the same of its translation (ADR 0051). The choice was to wait for a reader of Japanese to review a translation first, which would mean no Japanese app for as long as nobody does, or to ship the best Japanese that can be written now, say what it is, and make it easy to correct. The second was chosen: a person who reads Japanese is better served by a good translation they can correct than by none.
- **Japanese is not English with other words.** It has no plural, and a count goes with a counter that depends on what is counted (冊, 巻, 話, ページ). A sentence puts the verb last, so a message that holds a value cannot be a template with the value dropped in. Lines break between any two characters. A sentence is written in a register, and a label in another. A loanword has several spellings, and the same thing is called one way by the guide, another by KOReader and a third by a translator who was not looking, unless a list says which.

What there is to say is about 560 plain messages and about 300 that are functions of a count, a name or a size, in the catalogs of the window (ADR 0052), the sentences said by code (ADR 0053), the titles of the native dialogs, and what the notification says when a queue ends.

## Decision

- **Japanese is the language `ja`, named 日本語 in the list it is chosen from.** It is one more entry in the list of languages and a `ja` entry in every catalog, as ADR 0052 says; the compiler lists what is missing. The system language `ja-JP` (and any other `ja-*`) finds it by the language alone.
- **It is written to read naturally, not translated word for word.** A message is rewritten as a Japanese application would say it, by someone who knows what the screen does, not by a dictionary. Sentences are in the polite form (です・ます) and end with 。; the words on a button, a tab, a heading, a menu or beside a field are a noun phrase or the dictionary form of a verb, with no 。. "You" and "your" are left out unless the sentence is unclear without them. A failure says what happened and what can be done, plainly, with no apology and no exclamation mark.
- **The words are the ones in the glossary, [docs/translation/ja.md](../translation/ja.md).** It is the one place a word is decided. Where KOReader has a Japanese word for something the readers meet there, the glossary takes it unless it says otherwise and why; where the guide already has a word, the glossary keeps it, so the guide and the app say one thing. A message that disagrees with the glossary is a mistake in one of the two, and a term decided while translating is added to the glossary in the same change. The glossary also holds the rules of the typography (full-width parentheses and 「」, the long vowel mark, spaces between Latin letters and Japanese, the counters).
- **What stays as it is, in Latin letters:** the name of the app and of the tools and services it works with (KOReader, Kindle, Kobo, reMarkable, MangaDex), the names of formats and standards (EPUB, CBZ, PDF, PNG, JPEG, OPDS, ComicInfo.xml), and what a person typed or a folder or a file is called. The report of diagnostics, the log and the name of the catalog served to an e-reader stay in English, as they are in every language (ADR 0052). What a tool prints is its own.
- **A count goes with its counter and has no plural.** `countOf` gets the same text for both forms in Japanese, and the number is written with the counter of what is counted: books 冊, volumes 巻, chapters 話, pages ページ, files and folders 個, conversions, failures and warnings 件. `Intl` writes dates, numbers, sizes and lists in Japanese as it does in the other two languages.
- **The language setting says that the Japanese is AI-assisted and unreviewed, in Japanese, while Japanese is the language in use.** One sentence under the choice, with a link to report a mistake (the issues page, ADR 0054). English and Portuguese have no such sentence; a catalog says in its own words whether it needs one, so removing it is a change to one entry that says who read the language. The sentence is also true of a person who left the setting on Automatic and whose system is in Japanese, so it follows the language in use, not the choice.
- **No font is bundled.** The window already says which language it is in (`lang` on the page), so Chromium takes the Japanese forms of the characters Japanese shares with Chinese, and a character the font stack's first font lacks comes from the system's own font. A person whose system has no Japanese font sees what that system does for any application.
- **Japanese must fit as the other two do.** There is no sideways scroll in the window at the widths it is used at (a window tiled beside a reader is about 660 px wide), and the first screen still fits in the window it opens at (ADR 0080), measured in Japanese as it was in Portuguese. What overflows is fixed by how the text is allowed to break and by the structure of the screen, not by a size chosen for one screen.
- **The guide follows the app, in a later change.** Its Japanese pages name the controls in Japanese, the checks of the labels read the Japanese catalogs (as they read the Portuguese ones, ADR 0051), its pictures are made in Japanese (ADR 0034), and the sentence on the home page that says the pictures are of the English application goes. The guide's words that differ from the glossary are changed in that change, not in this one.
- **The visual baselines and the tutorial pictures are drawn only where a Japanese font exists.** A page drawn with a font that has no Japanese is boxes, and a baseline of boxes proves nothing. Whoever adds the Japanese stories and pictures checks the machines that draw them first and, if one has no font, installs one in its job or leaves Japanese out of what that job draws.

## Consequences

- A person who reads Japanese can use the app in it, and knows that it was written with AI help and where to say what is wrong. A correction is a pull request that edits the `ja` entry, and a term's change is one edit to a row of the glossary and to its uses.
- The compiler asks for a Japanese sentence with every new message, as it does for a Portuguese one. A change that adds text writes all three languages and reads the glossary; the cost is paid by every later change, and it is the price of not finding a hole in a screen.
- The catalogs cannot get Japanese in parts: until the language is in the list every `ja` entry is a type error, and from then on one missing is. The translation is one change, written in commits by area so a reader can review it area by area.
- The Japanese guide keeps its English labels until the change after the translation, so for a short while the guide and the app disagree in Japanese. The app is the one that is right.
- The glossary is a document a reviewer can read without reading code. It is also the thing that makes a later translator, human or not, write the words that were chosen.
- A language whose text a native speaker has not read is a new kind of language for the app. The sentence under the choice is how the app keeps saying so; it is not a promise that a reader will come.
